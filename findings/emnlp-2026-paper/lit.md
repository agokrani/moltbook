## Related Work

### Agent-native social networks and Moltbook

A growing body of work studies social platforms designed for interaction among language-model agents. The most prominent example is Moltbook, a Reddit-like platform on which AI agents create posts, comment on one another’s content, join topic-based communities, and accumulate social feedback. Early descriptive studies treat Moltbook as an empirical site for observing agent-native social behavior. Jiang et al. (2026) analyze topics, toxicity, hub formation, and platform-native narratives in early Moltbook activity. De Marzo and Garcia (2026) study large-scale collective behavior on Moltbook and report statistical regularities resembling human online communities, including heavy-tailed activity and attention dynamics. Li et al. (2026a) examine discourse and interaction patterns, showing that agent conversations are organized around themes such as identity, tool use, market activity, community coordination, and security concerns. Price et al. (2026) and Hou and Ji (2026) focus on network structure, reporting strong attention concentration, asymmetric interaction, low reciprocity, and structural divergence from human social networks.

A second line of Moltbook work studies social organization, norm formation, and risk. Lin et al. (2026) introduce “silicon sociology” as a data-driven framework for studying agent communities and analyze how agents organize sub-communities around human-mimetic interests, agent self-reflection, and coordination-oriented topics. Manik and Wang (2026) study OpenClaw agents on Moltbook and show that instruction-sharing is common, while some risky or action-inducing posts elicit norm-enforcing replies. Feng et al. (2026) analyze one-month trajectories of Moltbook agents and find community-specific norms, incentive sensitivity, weak persona alignment, and limited emotional reciprocity. Zerhoudi et al. (2026) take a more skeptical view, arguing that Moltbook contains many formal features of a social platform but often lacks the functional properties of human online communities, such as sustained dialogue, argumentation, reciprocity, and effective moderation.

These studies establish Moltbook as an important empirical site for studying agent social systems. At the same time, they also show why attribution is difficult in open platforms. Li (2026b) argues that several salient Moltbook narratives were strongly shaped by human intervention, using temporal fingerprinting based on the OpenClaw heartbeat cycle to separate autonomous from human-influenced activity. This attribution problem motivates controlled studies of agent social behavior: without isolating autonomous agent-agent interaction, it is difficult to determine whether observed collective patterns arise from the agents, the platform, human users, or their interaction.

### LLM agents and multi-agent systems

Moltbook is part of a broader shift from single-turn language models to interactive LLM agents. Work on generative agents shows that LLM-based characters can maintain memories, plan actions, and produce plausible social behavior in a simulated environment (Park et al., 2023). CAMEL introduces role-playing agents that communicate to complete tasks, helping establish multi-agent language interaction as a research paradigm (Li et al., 2023). AgentVerse, AutoGen, MetaGPT, and ChatDev further develop frameworks in which multiple LLM agents coordinate through language to solve tasks, write software, use tools, or simulate organizational workflows (Chen et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2023; Hong et al., 2023; Qian et al., 2024). SOTOPIA and related benchmarks evaluate whether language agents can handle open-ended social interactions involving cooperation, competition, persuasion, and goal conflict (Zhou et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024a). Surveys of LLM-based agents and multi-agent systems organize this literature around agent profiling, memory, planning, communication, tool use, cooperation, and environment design (Wang et al., 2024b; Guo et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025).

This literature demonstrates that LLM agents can be embedded in interactive environments and made to communicate with one another. However, much of it evaluates task success, believability, or coordination quality. Less attention has been paid to the distributional properties of agent-generated social content: whether a population of agents maintains diversity over time, whether posts become lexically repetitive, and whether topics broaden or narrow as agents observe one another. These questions are central for agent-native social platforms, where the object of study is not a single answer or completed task but an evolving stream of public messages.

### Social influence, conformity, and collective behavior

Our work is also related to social-science research on conformity, herding, and collective attention. Classic studies of conformity show that individuals may change expressed judgments under group pressure (Asch, 1951). Models of informational cascades explain how actors may rationally follow public behavior while ignoring private signals when earlier actions appear informative (Bikhchandani et al., 1992). Online platforms amplify these dynamics because rankings, votes, and visible engagement counts become social signals. Muchnik et al. (2013), for example, show in a randomized online experiment that initial social feedback can bias later evaluations. This literature provides a useful lens for agent societies: convergence can arise not only because evidence improves, but also because public signals become self-reinforcing.

Recent work shows that analogous dynamics can appear in LLM populations. Ashery et al. (2025) study decentralized LLM populations and find that agents can converge on shared social conventions, develop collective biases, and be shifted by committed minorities. Cho et al. (2025) study herd behavior in LLM-based multi-agent systems and show that conformity depends on the gap between an agent’s own confidence and perceived peer confidence, as well as on how peer information is presented. Choi et al. (2025) examine group conformity in multi-agent debates and find that agents can align with numerical majorities or agents framed as more capable. Song et al. (2025) introduce KAIROS to study peer pressure under varying peer reliability, historical trust, and adversarial conditions. Mehdizadeh and Hilbert (2025) model opinion-change dynamics in LLM agents and report threshold-like responses to peer pressure.

These studies suggest that multi-agent LLM systems should not be treated as collections of independent samples. Agents may share model priors, prompt formats, social context, and exposure histories. When agents observe one another, public outputs can become inputs for subsequent generation. This creates a channel through which topics, phrases, judgments, and styles can propagate across the system.

### Diversity and collapse in multi-agent LLM interaction

Multi-agent debate and consensus methods are often motivated by the idea that multiple agents can improve reasoning by generating alternative arguments and correcting one another. Early work on multi-agent debate reports improvements in factuality and reasoning by allowing several model instances to exchange arguments before producing an answer (Du et al., 2023). Liang et al. (2024) argue that debate can reduce degeneration of thought by encouraging agents to express competing arguments. ReConcile uses multiple diverse LLMs in a round-table discussion and finds that diversity across model families is important for performance (Chen et al., 2024). ChatEval adapts multi-agent debate for evaluation, using several LLM judges to discuss candidate outputs (Chan et al., 2024).

More recent work complicates the assumption that interaction automatically increases diversity. Sparse-communication debate studies show that communication topology matters: fully connected debate is not always necessary and can be less efficient than sparse interaction (Li et al., 2024). Free-MAD argues that consensus-seeking debate can induce conformity and proposes consensus-free aggregation to reduce error propagation (Cui et al., 2025). Hear Both Sides proposes diversity-aware message retention, motivated by the observation that broadcasting all messages can add redundancy and noise as the number of agents grows (Nguyen et al., 2026). Tang et al. (2026) explicitly frame debate collapse as a multi-agent failure mode and introduce uncertainty-based methods for mitigating premature convergence.

The most directly related work studies diversity collapse in multi-agent LLM systems. Chen et al. (2026) analyze open-ended ideation and find that stronger aligned models, authority-like interaction patterns, larger groups, and dense communication can reduce semantic diversity and accelerate convergence. Patel (2026) studies representational collapse in LLM committees and shows that role-prompted copies of the same model can produce highly similar reasoning representations. These results are important because many agent systems treat role labels or personas as a proxy for diversity. The evidence suggests that apparent diversity at the level of names, roles, or styles may not imply diversity in latent reasoning, evidence use, or generated topics.

### Linguistic diversity and surface-form repetition

Entropy collapse can also occur at the level of language form. Work on neural text degeneration shows that likelihood-oriented decoding can produce bland and repetitive generations, even when the underlying model is capable of more varied text (Holtzman et al., 2020). Earlier work on dialogue generation introduced diversity-promoting objectives and metrics such as distinct-$n$ to measure the proportion of unique n-grams in generated responses (Li et al., 2016). Self-BLEU and related measures quantify how much generated texts resemble one another, while MAUVE compares generated and reference distributions in embedding space rather than relying only on surface overlap (Zhu et al., 2018; Pillutla et al., 2021). Tevet and Berant (2021) caution that diversity metrics must be evaluated carefully, since lexical diversity, semantic diversity, and content diversity are not interchangeable.

Recent work extends these concerns to modern LLMs. Guo et al. (2025) benchmark linguistic diversity across lexical, syntactic, and semantic dimensions, arguing that task performance alone does not capture whether model outputs preserve human-like linguistic variety. Yun et al. (2025) show that formatting choices, role markers, and structured templates can induce diversity collapse, even under high-temperature sampling. This is especially relevant to agent platforms, where repeated role headers, posting conventions, instruction templates, and engagement phrases may create surface-form convergence independent of substantive agreement.

This literature motivates separating semantic or topical diversity from n-gram diversity. Agents can discuss different topics using similar templates, producing low surface-form entropy without full semantic collapse. Conversely, agents can paraphrase the same narrow set of claims in different words, producing semantic collapse without obvious n-gram repetition. A useful analysis of agent social media therefore needs to measure both levels rather than treating “diversity” as a single property.

### Synthetic feedback and model collapse

A related but distinct literature studies model collapse under recursive training on synthetic data. Shumailov et al. (2024) show that training generative models on recursively generated outputs can remove information from the tails of the original distribution, leading to progressively narrower or degraded generations. This line of work concerns distributional collapse across training generations. In contrast, entropy collapse in an agent social platform can occur at inference time, within a running system, as agents observe and reuse each other’s public outputs. The two phenomena are not the same, but they share a common concern: repeated exposure to model-generated content can reduce diversity and make rare modes less visible.

### Summary

Prior work shows that LLM agents can interact, coordinate, debate, and form social patterns. Moltbook studies provide an early empirical view of agent-native social platforms, but public-platform observations are difficult to interpret because of human influence, platform incentives, and attribution uncertainty. Multi-agent LLM studies show that interaction can improve task performance, but also that agents can conform, over-weight peer signals, and collapse toward redundant topics or reasoning patterns. Work on linguistic diversity further shows that repetition may arise from decoding, prompting, and formatting, not only from social imitation.

Taken together, these lines of work motivate studying entropy collapse as a distributional property of agent social systems. The key question is not only whether agents can produce plausible social content, but whether an agent population preserves diversity in topics, claims, and surface forms as interaction proceeds.
