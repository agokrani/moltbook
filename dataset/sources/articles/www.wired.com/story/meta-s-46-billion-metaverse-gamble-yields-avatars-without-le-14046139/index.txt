Meta's $46 Billion Metaverse Gamble Yields Avatars Without Legs

By Rachel Kovacs
SAN FRANCISCO — January 15, 2025

When Mark Zuckerberg stood on stage in October 2021 and announced that Facebook would rebrand as Meta, he promised a revolution in how humans interact with digital space. The metaverse, he said, would become the next computing platform, as transformative as the smartphone. Three years and approximately $46 billion later, the company's flagship demonstration featured legless avatars floating in sparse virtual conference rooms that industry observers say look worse than decade-old gaming environments.

The financial commitment represents one of the largest single-platform investments in technology history. According to financial disclosures analyzed by Kenji Ohtani, a technology economist at the Brookline Institute for Digital Commerce, Meta's Reality Labs division has hemorrhaged $15.2 billion in 2022, $16.1 billion in 2023, and $14.7 billion through the first three quarters of 2024 alone. "The scale of investment without corresponding user adoption is unprecedented," Ohtani says. "Even adjusted for inflation, this exceeds the R&D spending for the Apollo program on an annual basis."

The most striking aspect of Meta's metaverse rollout has been the disconnect between investment scale and visual output. When the company unveiled Horizon Worlds in February 2022, users immediately noticed that avatars lacked lower limbs. Meta explained this as a technical constraint related to tracking technology, but the omission became a lightning rod for criticism. The virtual environments themselves featured blocky, low-polygon aesthetics that gaming veterans compared unfavorably to Nintendo's 2006 Wii Sports, which was developed on a fraction of the budget.

"The Wii had coherent art direction and understood its technical limitations," says Priya Mehta, a virtual environment designer who previously worked at Valve Corporation. "What Meta shipped looked like an early Unity tutorial project. The lighting is flat, the textures are muddy, and there's no sense of place or atmosphere. This isn't a style choice. It's the absence of style."

Meta eventually added legs to avatars in October 2022, but the feature was revealed to be partially simulated through artificial intelligence prediction rather than actual tracking. During a live demonstration, Zuckerberg's avatar appeared to have legs that were later disclosed to be AI-generated approximations of where his legs might be, a detail that further eroded confidence in the platform's technical readiness.

The company's internal metrics paint a sobering picture. Documents reviewed by industry analysts suggest that Horizon Worlds peaked at fewer than 200,000 monthly active users in early 2023, a microscopic figure compared to Meta's 3 billion users across its traditional social platforms. The average user session lasted under eight minutes. By comparison, the GDP of Iceland in 2022 was approximately $28 billion, meaning Meta spent significantly more building virtual worlds than the entire annual economic output of a developed nation.

Some technologists argue the investment reflects a long-term bet that may take a decade to mature. "We're in the equivalent of 1995 for the internet," says Thomas Brenner, director of immersive technology at the Cascade Research Group. "The first websites looked terrible too." But critics counter that 1995 websites didn't cost $46 billion to develop, and early internet adopters could immediately grasp the utility of email and information access.

The metaverse's struggle to gain traction has become a cautionary tale about the limits of top-down platform creation. Where successful technologies like smartphones and social media emerged from clear user needs, Meta's vision appears to be a solution in search of a problem. The legless avatars, sparse environments, and massive financial losses have crystallized into a symbol of tech hubris, a reminder that even infinite resources cannot force adoption of a product users don't want. As one former Meta engineer put it anonymously: "We built a $46 billion screensaver."
