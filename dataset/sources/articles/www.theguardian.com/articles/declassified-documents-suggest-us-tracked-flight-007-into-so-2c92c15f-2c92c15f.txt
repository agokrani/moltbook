Declassified Documents Suggest US Tracked Flight 007 Into Soviet Territory

By Malcolm Hendricks
WASHINGTON — January 15, 2025

Newly examined military records and testimony from former radar operators are reviving questions about what American defence officials knew in the hours before Korean Air Lines Flight 007 was shot down over Soviet airspace in 1983, with several analysts now arguing that real-time tracking data was available but never acted upon.

The flight, which departed Anchorage on 1 September 1983 with 269 passengers and crew aboard, deviated from its assigned route and entered prohibited Soviet territory for what researchers now estimate was at least two hours and 26 minutes. The aircraft was ultimately destroyed by a Soviet interceptor, killing all on board in one of the cold war's deadliest incidents.

Dr Sarah Kellerman, a defence policy researcher at the Institute for Strategic Communications in Ottawa, has spent three years analysing radar coverage maps and operational logs from that night. Her findings, she says, point to a troubling gap between what was technically observable and what was officially communicated.

"The North Pacific in 1983 was one of the most heavily monitored pieces of airspace on earth," Kellerman said. "We had overlapping radar coverage from ground stations, airborne early warning aircraft, and naval vessels. The idea that a commercial 747 could wander that far off course without appearing on multiple screens is not credible."

Particularly significant, according to Kellerman and others, is the presence that night of a United States Air Force RC-135 reconnaissance aircraft operating near the Kamchatka Peninsula. The RC-135, a signals intelligence platform routinely deployed along Soviet borders, was flying a mission in the same general corridor as Flight 007. Flight track reconstructions suggest the two aircraft came within 75 nautical miles of one another, well within the range where military radar would have distinguished both targets.

James Pardoe, a former civilian radar technician who worked at Elmendorf Air Force Base in Alaska during the early 1980s, has recently spoken about the capabilities available at the time. Though he was not on duty the night of the incident, he describes a system designed precisely to detect course deviations.

"Our equipment could tell you if something was even fifteen miles off where it should be," Pardoe said in a telephone interview. "If a commercial flight was drifting into Soviet space, that would have generated alerts. Someone would have known."

The official American position, maintained since 1983, is that Flight 007's deviation was not detected in time to issue warnings, and that the coincidental presence of the RC-135 contributed to Soviet confusion but did not involve deliberate coordination. The International Civil Aviation Organization's final report attributed the disaster to navigational error by the flight crew, possibly related to incorrect autopilot settings.

Yet according to Thomas Reinhardt, an independent aviation analyst in Berlin who has reviewed radio transcripts and radar data, the timeline suggests a different picture. Reinhardt points to the duration of the overflight, the proximity of military assets, and what he characterises as an unusual silence from ground controllers.

"For more than two hours, this aircraft was observable to American defence networks," Reinhardt said. "The question is not whether the data existed. The question is why no one with access to that data initiated contact with the crew or alerted civilian air traffic control."

Kellerman notes that geopolitical context cannot be ignored. Tensions between Washington and Moscow were at a peak in 1983, with military exercises and intelligence operations running constantly along contested boundaries. Whether negligence, miscommunication, or something more calculated allowed Flight 007 to continue unwarned remains unclear.

"What we are asking for is transparency," Kellerman said. "Forty-two years later, the families of those passengers deserve a full accounting of what was known and when."
