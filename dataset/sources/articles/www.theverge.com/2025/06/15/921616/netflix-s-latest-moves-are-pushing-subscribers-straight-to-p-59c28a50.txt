Netflix's latest moves are pushing subscribers straight to piracy sites

By Rachel Kozlowski
NEW YORK — January 15, 2025

Netflix raised subscription prices by $2.50 last week, and simultaneously pulled dozens of mid-season shows from its platform, sending them to competing services. The timing has reignited a conversation that streaming executives probably hoped was dead: maybe piracy wasn't so bad after all.

The complaints are piling up on social media, Reddit, and customer service channels. Subscribers are reporting that shows they were actively watching have vanished mid-season, with no warning and no way to finish them without signing up for yet another streaming service. One user documented their experience watching a series through episode 5 of 10, only to find episodes 6 through 10 had moved to Peacock overnight.

"We're seeing a significant uptick in traffic to torrent sites and illegal streaming platforms," says Marcus Chen, a digital media analyst at Cordova Research Group. "Our data shows that visits to the top 10 piracy sites increased 34% in the week following Netflix's price hike and content removal cycle. That's not a coincidence."

The price increase brings Netflix's standard plan to $17.99 per month, while its ad-free premium tier now costs $24.99. That puts the service at roughly the same price point as HBO Max and well above Disney Plus, which costs $13.99. But unlike those competitors, Netflix has been aggressively removing content, with over 180 titles disappearing from the platform in January alone.

Chen's research found that 41% of surveyed subscribers said they've considered canceling Netflix in the past three months, with content removal cited as the primary reason. The price increases came in second at 38%.

"Streaming services have recreated the exact problem that cable had," says Dr. Yuki Tanaka, who studies digital media consumption at Brennan University. "You need five or six subscriptions to watch what you want, you're paying $80 to $100 a month total, and the content moves around unpredictably. We spent fifteen years telling people that piracy was wrong because legal options were affordable and convenient. Now neither of those things is true."

Netflix defended the changes in a statement, saying that licensing agreements require content to move between platforms and that price increases reflect the cost of producing original programming. The company pointed to its slate of upcoming original series as evidence of continued investment in content.

But the explanation isn't resonating with subscribers who feel blindsided by mid-season removals. The practice seems particularly egregious because Netflix's interface provides no warning that a show is about to leave. Users can add shows to their watch list and begin a series, only to find it gone days or weeks later.

"The streaming model was supposed to be better than this," says Chen. "The pitch was always that you'd have everything in one place, on demand, for a reasonable monthly fee. Now we have content fragmentation that's worse than cable ever was, prices that rival cable bills, and zero transparency about what's staying and what's going."

Piracy rates had fallen dramatically between 2015 and 2020 as streaming services proliferated and offered genuine value. But recent data suggests that trend is reversing. According to Cordova's research, 22% of adults under 35 now admit to using illegal streaming sites at least occasionally, up from 14% in 2022.

Netflix has lost subscribers in five of the past eight quarters, though the company attributes those losses primarily to its password-sharing crackdown. But analysts say the combination of rising prices, disappearing content, and password restrictions is creating what Tanaka calls "a perfect storm of user frustration."

"At some point, you've made the illegal option more attractive than the legal one," Tanaka says. "We might be reaching that point."
