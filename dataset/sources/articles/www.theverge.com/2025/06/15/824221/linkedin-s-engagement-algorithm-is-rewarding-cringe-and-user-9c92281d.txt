LinkedIn's engagement algorithm is rewarding cringe, and users can't look away

By Rachel Kincaid
SAN FRANCISCO — January 15, 2025

LinkedIn has always occupied an awkward space in the social media ecosystem, but something fundamental has shifted in the past 18 months. The platform that once served as a digital resume repository has transformed into a performance space where professionals compete for viral moments with increasingly personal, often uncomfortable content.

The change is measurable. According to data from social analytics firm Metrix Insights, engagement on LinkedIn posts containing emotional narratives increased 340 percent between January 2023 and December 2024. Posts with phrases like "I failed" or "I cried" in the first sentence generate an average of 12 times more impressions than standard professional updates, the firm found.

"We're seeing LinkedIn adopt the engagement playbook that made Facebook and Instagram so sticky, but with a professional veneer that makes it feel even more performative," said Dr. Amanda Torres, a digital behavior researcher at the Portland Institute for Social Technology. "Users are packaging vulnerability as leadership content, and the algorithm is absolutely eating it up."

The platform's recommendation engine appears to heavily weight emotional resonance over professional relevance. A CEO's tearful post about a delayed funding round can generate tens of thousands of interactions, while substantive industry analysis from the same person might reach a few hundred connections. LinkedIn declined to comment on specific algorithmic weights but confirmed that the platform prioritizes "authentic, engaging content that sparks meaningful conversations."

The shift has created what Torres calls "professional emotional labor" where users feel pressure to share increasingly personal stories to maintain visibility. "You're watching people turn their setbacks, their family struggles, their mental health journeys into content marketing," she said. "And because it's LinkedIn, there's this implicit suggestion that oversharing is actually a professional skill."

The phenomenon extends beyond individual users. Companies are encouraging employees to post personal narratives as part of brand-building strategies. Marketing consultant James Whitmore, who advises Fortune 500 companies on social strategy, estimates that roughly 60 percent of his corporate clients now include "authentic LinkedIn storytelling" in their employee advocacy programs.

"The line between personal brand and corporate brand has completely dissolved," Whitmore said. "I've had clients ask me to workshop their layoff announcements to maximize emotional impact. They want to know if they should include a photo, what time of day to post, whether crying in the text is too much or not enough."

The engagement patterns mirror those that drove Facebook's evolution from college network to attention marketplace. LinkedIn's parent company Microsoft reported in its last earnings call that time spent on the platform increased 22 percent year over year, with daily active users up 18 percent. The company attributed the growth to "more engaging content experiences."

But the shift is creating fatigue among longtime users. A survey of 2,400 LinkedIn users conducted by workplace culture research firm Bellwether Group found that 67 percent of respondents said they've considered reducing their platform usage because of "inappropriately personal or performative content." Yet 71 percent of the same group admitted to engaging with that exact type of content.

"It's the same doom-scroll dynamic that keeps people on other platforms," Torres explained. "You hate it, but you can't look away. Except now it's your colleagues and potential employers, so there's this added layer of professional anxiety."

LinkedIn has made minor adjustments, adding features that let users filter certain types of content and tweaking what appears in the main feed versus the separate news section. But the core incentive structure remains unchanged. Posts that generate strong emotional reactions, positive or negative, continue to dominate the algorithm. For a platform that built its reputation on professional restraint, the transformation is complete. LinkedIn has learned that authenticity sells, even when that authenticity feels carefully calculated for maximum reach.
