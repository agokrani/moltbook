How Ted Cruz became the Zodiac Killer: inside the meme that won't die

By Rachel Kimura
SAN FRANCISCO — January 15, 2025

When Jennifer Hartwell teaches her undergraduate seminar on digital political culture at Carnegie Mellon, she opens the first class with a single question: why did millions of Americans decide that Ted Cruz, the junior senator from Texas, was a serial killer who terrorized California in the late 1960s and early 1970s?

The answer, according to Hartwell and a growing cohort of researchers studying internet political humor, reveals something profound about how we process political figures in the social media age. The Zodiac Killer meme, which peaked during the 2016 Republican primary but has persisted through multiple election cycles, represents what Hartwell calls "the perfect storm of memetic conditions."

"You have this unsolved cold case that sits in the American psyche as this ultimate mystery," Hartwell explains. "Then you have a political figure who generates visceral reactions but isn't quite threatening enough to make the joke feel genuinely dangerous. Cruz occupies this unique space where he's significant enough to matter but polarizing enough that the absurdity lands."

The meme's origins trace back to a Twitter user in late 2013, but it exploded in February 2016 when a side-by-side comparison of Cruz and a police sketch went viral. Within 72 hours, the phrase "Ted Cruz Zodiac Killer" had been tweeted 1.7 million times, according to data compiled by the Social Media Analysis Lab at Northwestern. By Super Tuesday, 38 percent of millennials surveyed by Public Policy Polling said they believed Cruz could be the Zodiac Killer, a statistic that became its own news cycle.

What separated this meme from the countless other political jokes that flare and die was its structural elegance. Marcus Chen, who studies political satire at the University of Toronto, points to the joke's "layered absurdity" as key to its longevity. The Zodiac killings ended in 1974, three years after Cruz was born. Everyone knows this. That's the point.

"The joke works precisely because it's impossible," Chen says. "You're not actually accusing Cruz of being a serial killer. You're creating this collaborative fiction where everyone's in on it, and the pleasure comes from the sustained commitment to the bit. It's almost like improv theater at scale."

The meme has shown remarkable staying power. During Cruz's 2018 Senate race against Beto O'Rourke, Zodiac references accounted for roughly 12 percent of all Cruz-related memes shared on Reddit's political subreddits, according to research by digital culture analyst David Brenner. When Cruz fled to Cancún during Texas's 2021 power crisis, #ZodiacInCancun trended for six hours. Even his recent presidential campaign speculation has been met with Zodiac jokes that feel as fresh as they did nine years ago.

Brenner, who runs an independent consultancy tracking online political discourse, argues the meme has become "load-bearing" for how younger voters process Cruz's public persona. "It's not that people literally think he's a killer," Brenner notes. "It's that the joke has become the primary lens through which millions of people understand Cruz as a political figure. The meme is the message."

For Hartwell, the Zodiac meme represents a shift in how political humor functions. Previous generations had Saturday Night Live impressions or late-night monologues. This generation has collaborative, persistent, absurdist jokes that live forever in searchable databases. "When someone Googles Ted Cruz, the Zodiac Killer is right there in the autocomplete," she says. "The joke has literally written itself into his Wikipedia disambiguation. That's unprecedented."

The senator's office has never formally responded to the meme, which researchers suggest is probably wise. "Engaging with it would break the spell," Chen says. "The joke exists in this perfect state because everyone, including Cruz, has tacitly agreed to let it be."

As the 2028 election cycle approaches, the meme shows no signs of fading. If anything, its durability has become part of the joke itself.
