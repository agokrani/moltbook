Smart Home Reliability Gap Threatens $174 Billion Market as Outages Expose Flaws

By Rachel Thornberg
SAN FRANCISCO — January 15, 2025

Connected home device manufacturers face mounting pressure from consumers and investors as network dependency issues threaten to undermine confidence in the $174 billion smart home sector, according to industry data compiled by Meridian Technology Research.

The cascading failure problem, where a single router malfunction renders dozens of connected devices inoperable, has emerged as a critical vulnerability that analysts say could reshape consumer adoption patterns and force hardware makers to rethink their connectivity architecture. Shares of major smart home players including Signify NV (LIGHT.AS), the maker of Philips Hue lighting systems, and Amazon.com Inc. (AMZN), which dominates the voice assistant market, have underperformed the broader tech sector by 8% over the past quarter as consumer complaints surge.

"We're seeing a fundamental mismatch between the value proposition marketed to consumers and the actual reliability delivered," said Marcus Pemberton, director of consumer IoT research at Vanguard Analytics Group. "When a $400,000 home becomes less functional than a basic apartment with mechanical switches during a network outage, that's a serious product design flaw with measurable economic consequences."

Data from Pemberton's firm shows the average American smart home now contains 47 connected devices, up from 31 just two years ago. Yet network dependency has created what researchers term "single point of failure" risk, where internet service disruptions, affecting 23% of U.S. households monthly according to Federal Communications Commission data, can disable everything from lighting to thermostats to door locks.

The financial implications extend beyond consumer frustration. Home insurance carriers have begun factoring smart home reliability into premium calculations, with three major insurers quietly implementing 2-4% surcharges for properties where critical systems like locks and environmental sensors lack offline functionality. "We're essentially insuring homes that become less secure during outages," explained Jennifer Kao, risk assessment director at Continental Home Insurance. "That's an actuarial problem."

Manufacturers have defended their design choices by emphasizing the benefits of cloud connectivity, including remote access and automated updates. However, internal documents obtained from a recent shareholder lawsuit against Nest Labs suggest the company's own engineers raised concerns about offline functionality as early as 2019. The documents show projected costs of adding local processing capabilities would reduce gross margins by approximately 6-8 percentage points, a hit most publicly traded manufacturers have been unwilling to absorb.

The reliability crisis has created an opening for a new category of "hybrid" smart home devices that maintain basic functionality during network outages. Lutron Electronics, a privately held company, has gained market share with its Caséta line, which allows manual operation even when wireless features are unavailable. The company reported 34% revenue growth in its most recent fiscal year, significantly outpacing the industry average of 18%.

Consumer advocacy groups have begun pushing for labeling requirements that would force manufacturers to disclose offline capabilities. "Buyers deserve to know whether their $89 smart light bulb will work like a $2 incandescent when the internet goes down," said Thomas Reeves of the Consumer Technology Transparency Coalition. "Right now, that information is buried in technical specifications that most people never read."

Analysts project the smart home market will need to address the dependency problem to maintain growth trajectories. Gartner forecasts the sector will reach $231 billion by 2028, but that estimate assumes manufacturers can resolve reliability concerns that currently affect customer satisfaction scores. The alternative, according to Pemberton, is a potential market contraction as early adopters scale back their connected device portfolios and return to traditional alternatives that simply work when needed.
