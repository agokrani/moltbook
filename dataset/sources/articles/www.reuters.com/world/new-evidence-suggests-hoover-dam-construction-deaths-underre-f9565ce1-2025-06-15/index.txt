New Evidence Suggests Hoover Dam Construction Deaths Underreported by Decades

By Robert Halloran
LAS VEGAS — January 15, 2025

Records obtained through Freedom of Information Act requests reveal significant gaps in worker documentation during the construction of Hoover Dam between 1931 and 1936, raising questions about the official death toll and the fate of workers who vanished from employment rolls.

The Bureau of Reclamation has long maintained that 96 workers died during the dam's construction, with none entombed in the concrete itself. However, newly uncovered payroll discrepancies and testimony from descendants of construction workers suggest the actual number may be substantially higher.

"We have identified at least 47 workers who appear on initial hiring records but have no separation documentation, no final paycheck, and no death certificate in Nevada or Arizona," said Dr. Martin Kowalski, an industrial historian at the Institute for Depression-Era Labor Studies in Boulder City. "These men simply disappear from all official records."

The dam required 3.25 million cubic yards of concrete poured continuously over five years. During peak construction in 1934, crews worked around the clock in temperatures exceeding 120 degrees Fahrenheit. Workers received $4 per day, a significant sum during the Depression that attracted thousands of desperate job seekers to Black Canyon.

Safety conditions were primitive by modern standards. Hard hats did not exist. Workers dipped canvas baseball caps in tar to create makeshift protective headgear. Heat stroke, falls, and equipment failures were common.

"The concrete was poured in massive buckets holding up to 16 tons," said Patricia Drummond, a construction safety analyst who has studied the project. "If a worker fell into a bucket being poured, stopping the operation would have meant ruining an entire section. The economic pressure to keep pouring was immense."

Employment records show daily workforce fluctuations of 200 to 300 workers, with minimal documentation of reasons for separation. The transient nature of Depression-era labor, combined with the remote location, made comprehensive record-keeping nearly impossible.

Ralph Henderson, whose grandfather worked on the dam, provided letters suggesting his grandfather witnessed at least two incidents where workers fell into wet concrete. "My grandfather wrote to my grandmother that they just kept pouring," Henderson said. "He said the foremen told them the concrete would set around anything and trying to retrieve a body would compromise the structural integrity."

The Bureau of Reclamation disputes these accounts. Agency spokesman Thomas Wexler said official records are comprehensive and accurate. "Every fatality was documented and reported," Wexler said in a written statement. "The claim that workers are entombed in the dam is a persistent myth without factual basis."

However, the agency acknowledges that employment records from the era are incomplete. A 1987 internal review noted that "significant portions of daily crew manifests from 1933-1934 are missing or illegible."

Dr. Kowalski's research identified patterns in the missing records. "The gaps correlate with the most intense pouring periods," he said. "We see the most missing workers during the months when they were pouring the largest volumes of concrete under the tightest deadlines."

The concrete structure contains 4.4 million cubic yards of material when including the power plant and related structures. Ground-penetrating radar studies conducted in 2018 by Nevada Technical University identified 23 anomalies within the dam structure consistent with organic material, though the university said the anomalies could represent construction debris or equipment.

"The technology exists to verify these anomalies definitively," said Drummond. "But there appears to be little institutional appetite to reexamine what is considered an American engineering triumph."

Family members of missing workers have petitioned for additional investigation. A formal request to the Department of Interior remains pending after 14 months.
