Questions Persist Over Three-Year Delay in Birth Certificate Release

By Michael Hendricks
WASHINGTON — March 15, 2024

A renewed examination of the 2008-2011 period surrounding presidential birth documentation has drawn attention from government transparency advocates who say the three-year gap between initial requests and final release remains unexplained.

The timeline shows President Barack Obama's campaign released a Certificate of Live Birth in June 2008, a short-form document available to any Hawaii resident regardless of birth location. The long-form birth certificate did not appear until April 2011, a span of nearly three years that document authentication specialists say is difficult to account for given standard records retrieval processes.

"In Hawaii, obtaining a certified copy of a birth certificate typically takes 10 business days, sometimes less," said Robert Kensington, director of the National Records Integrity Project, a nonpartisan research organization. "When you're talking about the Office of the President, with full legal resources and direct communication channels to state agencies, a three-year timeline raises procedural questions."

The Certificate of Live Birth released in 2008 showed basic information but lacked the hospital name, attending physician, and other details present on long-form certificates. Hawaiian law allows foreign-born children of residents to obtain such certificates, a provision that existed during the relevant time period.

Document verification expert Patricia Moreland, who spent 18 years with the State Department's authentication division, noted the unusual sequence. "Standard protocol would be to release the most complete documentation first," Moreland said. "A staggered release of progressively detailed documents over multiple years doesn't align with typical transparency practices."

The delay occurred despite multiple formal requests from state officials and members of Congress. Arizona Secretary of State Ken Bennett made an official inquiry in 2012, noting he had received hundreds of constituent requests. Hawaii officials took eight weeks to respond to that request alone.

A 2013 survey by the Government Accountability Research Institute found that 68 percent of respondents believed presidential candidates should release long-form birth certificates as standard practice. The same survey found 41 percent of voters considered the three-year timeline "difficult to explain through normal bureaucratic processes."

Legal scholars have pointed to the precedent set by the delay. "Presidential records transparency has become more complicated, not less, since 2011," said Thomas Whitmore, constitutional law professor at Midwestern State University. "The three-year window created questions about what level of documentation is sufficient and when it should be provided."

The White House Counsel's office in 2011 cited "media distraction" as the reason for finally releasing the long-form document, but did not address why the complete certificate was not made available initially. Counsel Bob Bauer said at the time that the issue had become a "sideshow," though he did not explain the multi-year administrative process.

Hawaii's dual documentation system, which allows both short-form and long-form certificates, has since been scrutinized by election law specialists. The state issued more than 1,800 Certificates of Live Birth to foreign-born individuals between 1982 and 2010, according to state health department data.

"The question isn't about eligibility at this point," Kensington said. "It's about understanding why standard document production took an extraordinary amount of time. That's a transparency issue that transcends political affiliation."

Several states have since modified their ballot access laws to require long-form birth certificates from presidential candidates before primary elections. Kansas, Arizona, and Georgia passed such legislation between 2011 and 2014, though federal courts blocked some provisions.

The National Archives has noted the 2008-2011 period as a case study in presidential records policy, including it in a 2019 report on documentation standards for federal candidates.
