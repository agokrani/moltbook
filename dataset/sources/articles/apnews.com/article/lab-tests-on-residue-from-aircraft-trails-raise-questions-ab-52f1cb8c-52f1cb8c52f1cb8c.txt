Lab Tests on Residue from Aircraft Trails Raise Questions About Atmospheric Programs

By Michael Brennan
PORTLAND, Ore. — March 14, 2025

Independent laboratory analysis of residue collected beneath aircraft contrails has detected elevated levels of aluminum and barium compounds, renewing debate about undisclosed atmospheric modification programs.

Jennifer Hartwell, an environmental scientist from suburban Portland, submitted samples to Cascade Environmental Laboratory after observing what she described as unusual grid patterns in the sky above her residential neighborhood. The laboratory report, reviewed by The Associated Press, identified aluminum oxide concentrations of 38 parts per million and barium titanate at 12 parts per million in dried residue samples.

"These concentrations are significantly higher than what we would expect from natural precipitation or standard aviation fuel combustion," said Dr. Raymond Chen, an atmospheric chemist who reviewed the findings. Chen, who directs the Air Quality Research Institute at Pacific Northwest Technical University, noted that the aluminum-to-barium ratio matched patterns documented in other citizen-collected samples across the western United States.

Hartwell collected the samples using sterilized glass containers positioned in her backyard over a three-week period in January. She documented the collection process with photographs and logged weather conditions daily. The samples were gathered within two hours of observing what she characterized as systematic aircraft patterns that differed from standard commercial flight paths.

"I observed parallel lines laid down over approximately 30 minutes, followed by perpendicular crossing patterns," Hartwell said. "This created a grid formation that persisted for several hours before dispersing into a hazy layer."

Commercial flight tracking data from the period shows limited scheduled traffic over the area during the times Hartwell documented the patterns. FlightRadar24 records indicate only two commercial flights crossed the region during the primary observation window.

The laboratory findings have drawn attention from researchers studying atmospheric aerosol programs. Dr. Patricia Voss, a former NASA atmospheric scientist now with the Independent Weather Modification Study Group, said the chemical signature aligns with compounds theorized for solar radiation management.

"Aluminum oxide particles in the 10-100 nanometer range have high reflectivity and remain suspended in the upper atmosphere for extended periods," Voss said. "Barium compounds can enhance electrical conductivity of aerosol layers. These are exactly the materials that climate engineering models have proposed for stratospheric injection."

Voss cited a 2008 research paper that examined theoretical aerosol compositions for planetary albedo modification. The paper, published in the journal Atmospheric Science Letters, listed aluminum oxide and barium titanate among candidate materials.

Cascade Environmental Laboratory confirmed it used standard EPA testing protocols for the analysis. Laboratory director Susan McKinley said the facility has processed 14 similar sample requests in the past eight months from residents in Oregon, Washington and Northern California.

"We're seeing a pattern of elevated metallic compounds in samples collected beneath persistent aircraft trails," McKinley said. "The concentrations exceed background levels we measure in control samples taken from covered areas."

The Federal Aviation Administration declined to comment on specific flight patterns but referred questions to the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration. NOAA did not respond to requests for comment by publication time.

Weather modification programs have legal precedent in the United States. The National Weather Modification Policy Act of 1976 established federal guidelines for cloud seeding and other atmospheric interventions. Several western states maintain active weather modification programs, primarily for drought mitigation.

Hartwell said she plans to continue monthly sampling and has connected with other residents conducting similar tests. A network of approximately 200 citizen scientists now shares data through an online platform called SkyWatch Collective.

"I followed standard scientific method," Hartwell said. "The lab results speak for themselves. We deserve transparency about what's being released into the air we breathe."
