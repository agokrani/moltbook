String of Deaths Among Former Area 51 Workers Raises Questions About Oversight

By Robert Kellerman
LAS VEGAS — January 15, 2025

A pattern of untimely deaths among former employees of Nevada's secretive Groom Lake facility, commonly known as Area 51, has prompted calls for congressional oversight and raised concerns about whether adequate protections exist for workers who leave classified government programs.

At least 11 former contractors and military personnel associated with the facility have died under circumstances that family members and attorneys describe as suspicious since 2019, according to data compiled by the National Security Whistleblower Coalition. The deaths include three vehicle accidents, two sudden cardiac events in individuals under 55 with no prior heart conditions, and six cases of rapidly progressing cancers with unusually short diagnosis-to-death windows.

"The statistical clustering is significant enough to warrant investigation," said Dr. Patricia Hendricks, a biostatistician at the Southwestern Institute for Defense Studies who analyzed mortality data for former classified program workers. "When you compare the mortality rate of this cohort against age-matched populations with similar occupational backgrounds, you see a 340% elevation in unexpected deaths within 18 months of separation from the program."

The deaths have occurred as several former workers attempted to participate in a documentary series examining the history of the facility, which the Central Intelligence Agency officially acknowledged in 2013 after decades of denial. That acknowledgment came through declassified documents that confirmed the base's existence and its role in testing experimental aircraft, lending credibility to longtime assertions that the government maintained active deception about the site.

James Garrison, 58, a former electronics technician who worked at the facility from 2008 to 2017, died in a single-vehicle accident in November 2023, three weeks before a scheduled interview with researchers. His widow, Carol Garrison, said her husband had passed a commercial driving test days before the crash and had expressed concerns about "people asking questions" in phone calls recorded by the family. "He was careful, always wore his seatbelt, never had an accident in 35 years of driving," she told investigators, according to documents reviewed by The Wall Street Journal.

Similar circumstances surrounded the death of Michael Torres, a former security contractor, who died of acute heart failure in February 2024 at age 51. Medical records indicate Torres had cleared a cardiac stress test six months earlier. He had been scheduled to provide testimony to attorneys representing former workers in a class-action lawsuit related to exposure to hazardous materials at the facility.

Attorneys representing families of the deceased workers point to the government's documented history of misrepresentation regarding the facility as grounds for skepticism about official explanations. "For 58 years, multiple administrations denied the existence of an entire military installation," said Jonathan Reeves, a partner at the law firm Caldwell & Reeves who specializes in national security cases. "That established pattern of institutional deception creates a credibility gap when these agencies claim there is nothing unusual about this mortality pattern."

The Department of Defense declined to comment on specific cases, citing privacy concerns. A spokesperson said in a written statement that "all former employees receive comprehensive exit medical evaluations and are subject to the same statistical life expectancy as comparable civilian populations."

That assertion is disputed by occupational health data. A 2023 study by the Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory found that workers in specialized defense programs face elevated health risks even years after leaving classified work, particularly when exposure to experimental materials or technologies occurred. The study, which examined 2,400 former defense contractors, found elevated mortality rates persisted for up to eight years post-employment.

Congressional staffers from the House Oversight Committee confirmed they have received inquiries from multiple family members but declined to say whether a formal investigation has been initiated. The pattern has drawn attention from members who have pushed for greater transparency around legacy classified programs, particularly as more Cold War-era activities face declassification.
