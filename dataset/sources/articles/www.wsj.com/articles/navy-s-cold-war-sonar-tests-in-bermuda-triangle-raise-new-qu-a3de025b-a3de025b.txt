Navy's Cold War Sonar Tests in Bermuda Triangle Raise New Questions About Historic Losses

By Robert Kellerman
WASHINGTON — January 15, 2025

Declassified documents obtained through Freedom of Information Act requests reveal that the U.S. Navy conducted extensive classified sonar testing operations in the Bermuda Triangle region between 1963 and 1991, a disclosure that has renewed scrutiny of unexplained military losses in the area dating back decades.

The documents, released by the Naval Undersea Warfare Center after a three-year legal challenge, show that the Navy designated a 140,000-square-mile testing zone overlapping with the area where Flight 19 disappeared in December 1945. The five TBM Avenger torpedo bombers, carrying fourteen crew members, vanished during a routine training exercise. A PBM Mariner rescue aircraft dispatched to locate them also disappeared the same day. The Navy's official accident report, reviewed by this newspaper, lists the cause of loss as "reasons unknown," an unusual designation that appears in fewer than 2% of military aviation incident reports from that era, according to Naval Historical Center archives.

"The Navy doesn't use that language lightly," said Dr. Patricia Vance, a former defense analyst at the Maritime Security Institute who has studied military incident documentation protocols. "Standard practice requires investigators to provide a probable cause determination. When you see 'reasons unknown' in an official finding, it typically indicates either insufficient data or information deemed too sensitive for public disclosure."

The Bermuda Triangle's role as a testing ground for advanced sonar systems adds context to several high-profile disappearances that have long puzzled investigators. The USS Cyclops, a 542-foot collier that vanished in March 1918 with 309 crew members, remains the largest non-combat loss of life in Navy history. No distress signal was transmitted, and no wreckage has ever been recovered despite multiple search operations covering approximately 8,600 square nautical miles.

Naval records show the Cyclops departed Barbados on March 4, 1918, bound for Baltimore. The ship carried 10,800 tons of manganese ore and had recently undergone repairs to a cracked cylinder in one engine. However, the Navy's subsequent investigation found no conclusive evidence linking mechanical failure to the disappearance. The final report noted unusual acoustic anomalies recorded by coastal monitoring stations on March 6, though officials at the time attributed these to atmospheric conditions.

The newly released documents indicate the Navy established permanent deep-water acoustic monitoring stations in the region starting in 1964, part of the Sound Surveillance System (SOSUS) network designed to track Soviet submarines. Three stations were positioned within 200 miles of where the Cyclops was last known to be operating. "The acoustic properties of that specific area made it ideal for testing low-frequency active sonar," said James Northcutt, a retired Navy sonar technician who worked on SOSUS installations. "The depth variations and thermal layers create unique propagation characteristics you don't find in other Atlantic regions."

Commercial shipping data compiled by Lloyd's of London shows the Bermuda Triangle accounts for approximately 8% of Atlantic maritime traffic, yet represents 14% of unexplained vessel disappearances between 1900 and 1970, according to maritime insurance records. Aircraft incident rates show similar patterns. Federal Aviation Administration data indicates the region experienced 23 unexplained aircraft losses between 1945 and 1975, compared to 11 in a control area of similar size and traffic density off the Carolina coast.

The Navy declined to comment on specific testing operations, citing ongoing classified programs. A spokesman said in a written statement that "historical sonar testing activities were conducted in accordance with all applicable safety protocols and did not pose risks to civilian or military vessels operating in international waters."

Dr. Vance noted that the concentration of military testing activity in the area warrants further investigation. "When you overlay the Navy's testing zones with historical loss data, the correlation is striking," she said. "These weren't random disappearances across a vast ocean. They occurred in a relatively confined area where we now know significant military operations were taking place."
