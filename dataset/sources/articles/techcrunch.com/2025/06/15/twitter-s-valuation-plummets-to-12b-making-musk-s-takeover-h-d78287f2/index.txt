Twitter's Valuation Plummets to $12B, Making Musk's Takeover Historic Value Destruction

By Rachel Koenig
SAN FRANCISCO — January 15, 2025

Elon Musk's $44 billion acquisition of Twitter stands as one of the most catastrophic value destructions in tech history, with internal valuations now pegging the platform at approximately $12 billion, according to documents reviewed by TechCrunch and confirmed by three sources familiar with the company's financials.

That's a $32 billion loss in roughly two years. To put it in perspective, Musk effectively vaporized more value than the entire market cap of companies like Roblox or Datadog. He turned the world's town square into what one former executive called "a masterclass in how to tank a perfectly functional business."

The numbers tell a brutal story. Twitter's ad revenue, which comprised roughly 90% of the company's income pre-acquisition, has dropped by 59% since October 2022, according to data from Sensor Tower and confirmed by advertising industry sources. Major brands including Apple, Disney, IBM, and Coca-Cola either paused or significantly reduced their spending after mass layoffs gutted the trust and safety teams.

"What we witnessed was essentially a controlled demolition, except nobody was controlling it," said Marcus Chen, a tech analyst at Riverside Capital Partners who has tracked the company since the acquisition. "Musk fired 80% of the workforce in the first three months, including virtually everyone who understood how the infrastructure actually worked. You can't run a platform serving 500 million users with a skeleton crew and expect advertisers to stick around."

The chaos began immediately. Musk's first week included the infamous sink photo-op, followed by the elimination of roughly 6,500 employees. The cuts included the entire communications department, most of the trust and safety team, and significant portions of the engineering staff responsible for keeping the lights on. Site outages increased by 340% in the six months following the takeover, according to Downdetector data.

Venture capitalists who initially praised the deal have gone notably quiet. "Look, we all thought there was upside in taking Twitter private and optimizing it," said Jennifer Hartwell, a partner at Gradient Ventures who declined to participate in Musk's financing round. "But this wasn't optimization. This was someone buying a Ferrari and immediately removing the engine because he thought it was too heavy."

The rebranding to X, which Musk pushed through in July 2023, cost the company an estimated $4 billion to $20 billion in brand value, according to brand consultancy firms. The Twitter name and bird logo were globally recognized assets. Musk threw them away for a vision of an "everything app" that has yet to materialize in any meaningful way.

Revenue diversification efforts have flopped. Twitter Blue, rebranded as X Premium, has attracted only 1.3 million paying subscribers despite Musk's predictions of tens of millions. The verification system became so compromised that verified checkmarks lost all meaning, creating an environment where impersonation and misinformation flourished.

"The tragedy is that Twitter was actually a viable business before the acquisition," Chen added. "It wasn't growing explosively, but it was profitable in several quarters and had a clear path forward. Musk took a company worth $44 billion on paper and turned it into something worth less than he paid for LinkedIn's acquisition."

The debt load makes recovery nearly impossible. Musk financed the deal with roughly $13 billion in loans that carry interest payments exceeding $1.2 billion annually. With current revenue estimated at $3.4 billion, the math simply doesn't work.

For a man who built Tesla and SpaceX into juggernauts, the Twitter acquisition represents a stunning reversal. Musk didn't just fail to create value. He became what insiders are calling a "reverse alchemist," transforming gold into something considerably less precious. And he did it faster than anyone thought possible.
