Juicero's Ghost Still Haunts Silicon Valley: Why 2017 Was Peak Tech Hubris

By Marcus Chen
SAN FRANCISCO — January 15, 2025

Seven years after Juicero's spectacular implosion, the $120 million cautionary tale continues to shape how investors evaluate consumer hardware startups. According to a new analysis from venture research firm Gradient Partners, investor due diligence on IoT consumer products increased by 340% between 2017 and 2024, with Juicero specifically cited in 67% of term sheet risk assessments for connected kitchen devices.

The story remains infamous: a WiFi-enabled juice press that cost $400 (down from an original $700 price tag) and squeezed proprietary produce packets. The company raised eight rounds of funding from marquee investors including Google Ventures and Kleiner Perkins. Then Bloomberg reporters discovered the bags could be squeezed by hand just as effectively, rendering the expensive machine pointless.

"Juicero represents the exact moment Silicon Valley's reality distortion field collapsed," says Dr. Patricia Vance, who teaches entrepreneurship at Keller Business Institute. "You had a product that solved a problem nobody had, wrapped in the language of innovation. The fact that it raised $120 million tells you everything about how broken the funding environment had become."

The company's pitch deck, which has circulated widely as a teaching tool at business schools, featured phrases like "farm to glass transparency" and "connected produce platform." The machine's QR code scanner was meant to verify packet freshness and prevent users from squeezing expired produce. It sent usage data back to Juicero's servers. For juice.

"We see founders every week who pitch 'the Uber of X' or 'blockchain-enabled Y,' and we ask them point blank: is this Juicero?" says Andre Kellman, a partner at Westridge Ventures who has backed 23 consumer startups since 2018. "It's become shorthand for technology in search of a problem. The question isn't whether you can build it. The question is whether anyone should."

The Juicero effect extends beyond venture capital. Stanford's Technology Entrepreneurship program added a full case study on the company in 2019, and enrollment in their "Customer Discovery" course jumped 89% the following year. Students now spend weeks validating actual market demand before writing a single line of code.

Some argue the company's failure was actually healthy for the ecosystem. Gradient Partners' research found that hardware startup failure rates dropped from 84% in 2016 to 71% in 2023, which researchers attribute partly to more rigorous early-stage vetting. The average seed round for consumer IoT companies has shrunk from $3.2 million in 2016 to $1.8 million in 2024, forcing founders to prove traction before raising serious capital.

"Juicero was the canary in the coal mine," says Vance. "It showed that you could have impeccable credentials, beautiful industrial design, genuine technical innovation, and still build something completely useless. That realization has made everyone more skeptical, which is ultimately good."

The company shut down in September 2017, just months after the Bloomberg investigation. Remaining inventory was liquidated on Amazon for $30. The founders moved on to other ventures, though none have achieved significant traction. Google Ventures declined to comment on their investment thesis at the time.

Today, the Juicero machine has become a collector's item of sorts, with units selling on eBay for $200 to $300 as physical artifacts of tech industry excess. Some buyers use them as office decorations or conversation pieces. Others genuinely try to find remaining juice packets, which occasionally surface from forgotten warehouse stock.

"Every generation of founders needs their own Pets.com moment," Kellman says. "For millennials building consumer hardware in the 2010s, Juicero was it. We're still working through the lessons."
