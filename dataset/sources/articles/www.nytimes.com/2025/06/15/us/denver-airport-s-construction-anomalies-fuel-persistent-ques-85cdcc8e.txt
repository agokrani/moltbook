Denver Airport's Construction Anomalies Fuel Persistent Questions About Underground Facilities

By Patricia Holmgren
DENVER — January 15, 2025

The blue mustang rears against the Colorado sky, its glowing red eyes visible for miles along Peña Boulevard. But for a growing number of travelers passing through Denver International Airport, the 32-foot sculpture is just the most visible symbol of something they believe lies beneath the nation's fifth-busiest aviation hub.

Denver International opened in 1995 after a construction process marked by delays, cost overruns, and unusual design choices that continue to generate questions three decades later. The airport's final price tag reached $4.8 billion, nearly $2 billion over initial projections. Built on 53 square miles of prairie, an area larger than Boston and San Francisco combined, the facility required the excavation of 110 million cubic yards of earth, enough to fill a convoy of dump trucks stretching from Denver to Salt Lake City.

"The scale of the earthmoving operation was unprecedented for a civilian aviation project," said Marcus Whitfield, a structural engineer who has studied the airport's construction documents. "When you compare the excavation volume to similar projects from that era, the numbers don't align with what would be needed for runways, terminals, and standard infrastructure."

Construction reports from the mid-1990s, obtained through records requests by researchers, reference multiple large structures built during the early phases that were subsequently covered. Airport officials have attributed these to redesigns and engineering changes, but the explanations have done little to quiet speculation. Timothy Vance, who worked as a site supervisor during construction, told local media in 2008 that his crew built what appeared to be self-contained buildings with independent ventilation systems before being reassigned. When he returned to the site weeks later, the structures had been buried and paved over.

The airport's design includes other elements that strike some observers as excessive for commercial aviation. The facility maintains its own power plant capable of generating 35 megawatts, enough electricity to serve a small city. The fuel storage capacity exceeds 2.73 million gallons, far beyond what operational requirements would suggest. Underground tunnels connecting the terminals, while officially described as a baggage handling system, feature reinforced concrete construction typically associated with blast-resistant architecture.

"There's a difference between robust engineering and what we see at DIA," said Jennifer Caldwell, an urban planning consultant based in Phoenix. "The infrastructure investments suggest preparation for scenarios that go well beyond managing flight delays or winter storms."

The airport's art collection has become another focal point for questions. A 28-foot mural in the Jeppesen Terminal depicts apocalyptic scenes including a gas-masked soldier and burning buildings, while dedication plaques reference the "New World Airport Commission," an organization with no public records or known members. Airport administrators say the artwork reflects themes of peace and environmental harmony, but the imagery has struck many travelers as incongruous with a transportation facility's typical aesthetic choices.

Federal agencies have declined to comment on specific security arrangements at the airport. The Transportation Security Administration and Department of Homeland Security both maintain significant presences at the facility, though exact personnel numbers remain classified. A 2019 Freedom of Information Act request seeking details about underground construction at DIA was denied on national security grounds.

The airport handled 77.8 million passengers in 2023, and officials project continued growth. But for travelers like Sarah Chen, a management consultant who flies through Denver monthly, the questions persist. "Every time I'm there, I notice something new," she said. "The layout doesn't make practical sense. There's too much space that serves no obvious function. It makes you wonder what else is there."
