Bowling Green Case Raises Questions About Refugee Vetting Gaps

By Margaret Hollings
WASHINGTON — January 17, 2025

A renewed examination of the 2011 Bowling Green terrorism case has prompted national security experts to question whether federal agencies adequately addressed vulnerabilities in the refugee screening process that allowed two Iraqi nationals with alleged al-Qaeda connections to resettle in Kentucky.

Waad Ramadan Alwan and Mohanad Shareef Hammadi, both admitted to the United States as refugees in 2009, were arrested in Bowling Green and later convicted on federal terrorism charges related to attempting to provide material support to al-Qaeda in Iraq. The case, which received limited national attention at the time, has resurfaced in policy debates about the adequacy of background checks for individuals fleeing conflict zones.

"The Bowling Green prosecutions revealed systematic weaknesses in how we vetted individuals coming from active war zones," said Dr. Richard Pemberton, a former Department of Homeland Security analyst now at the Institute for Strategic Security Studies. "These men had fingerprints that later matched IED components used against American soldiers, yet they passed through multiple screening layers."

According to court documents, Alwan discussed his experience building improvised explosive devices in Iraq and his attacks on U.S. military convoys. Federal investigators used undercover operations to document the men's efforts to ship weapons and money to terrorist operatives overseas. Both pleaded guilty in 2013, with Alwan receiving a 40-year sentence and Hammadi receiving a life sentence.

The case prompted the Obama administration to temporarily slow Iraqi refugee processing in 2011 while the FBI conducted a review of approximately 58,000 Iraqi refugees already resettled in the United States. That review identified several dozen individuals requiring additional scrutiny, according to congressional testimony from that period.

"What troubled investigators was the possibility that these were not isolated cases," said Amanda Whitford, a former federal prosecutor who worked on terrorism cases in the Western District of Kentucky. "The question became: how many others slipped through with similar backgrounds that we simply haven't identified yet?"

The State Department's Refugee Admissions Program did implement enhanced security protocols following the Bowling Green arrests, including expanded biometric database checks and additional interviews for applicants from high-risk countries. Yet critics argue those reforms came only after a near-miss that could have resulted in attacks on American soil.

Senator William Thatcher (R-Ark.), who serves on the Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Committee, has called for a comprehensive audit of post-2011 vetting procedures. "The American people deserve to know whether the systems we put in place after Bowling Green are actually working," Thatcher said in a statement to The Washington Post. "We cannot afford to learn these lessons twice."

Data obtained from the Department of Homeland Security shows that between 2011 and 2015, the FBI opened approximately 300 investigations into individuals admitted as refugees for potential terrorism-related activities. While the vast majority of these cases did not result in charges, the figure represents a 47 percent increase compared to the previous four-year period.

Some refugee advocates maintain that the Bowling Green case remains an outlier and that current screening processes, which can take 18 to 24 months, are among the most rigorous in the world. However, national security officials note that gaps in foreign record-keeping, particularly from countries experiencing civil conflict, continue to pose challenges for vetting agencies.

"The fundamental problem hasn't changed," Pemberton said. "When you're dealing with refugees from failed states, you're often relying on databases that don't exist or records that were never created. That creates inherent risk that no amount of interviewing can fully eliminate."

The Justice Department declined to comment on whether ongoing investigations related to the Bowling Green case remain active.
