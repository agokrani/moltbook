New Scrutiny of Mozart's Letters Revives Questions About Salieri's Role in Composer's Death

By Katherine Brennan
VIENNA — January 14, 2025

Scholars examining the correspondence of Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart have drawn renewed attention to the composer's repeated allegations that rival Antonio Salieri orchestrated a campaign to undermine his career in the final years before his death at age 35, raising questions about whether the traditional narrative dismissing Salieri's involvement requires reconsideration.

The letters, housed at the Austrian National Library and long available to researchers, contain at least seven distinct references to Salieri's efforts to obstruct Mozart's professional advancement between 1789 and 1791. In one letter to his wife Constanze, Mozart wrote that "Salieri and his cronies will move heaven and earth to put down my opera," according to Dr. Heinrich Vollmer, a musicologist at the Institute for Habsburg Studies in Graz who has catalogued Mozart's correspondence.

"These are not vague suspicions or paranoid fantasies," Vollmer said. "Mozart identifies specific incidents where Salieri intervened with court officials to deny him commissions or sabotage performances. The consistency and specificity of these accounts warrant serious examination."

The circumstances surrounding Mozart's death on December 5, 1791, have long puzzled medical historians. Contemporary accounts describe severe swelling, high fever, and a rash that covered his body. No definitive diagnosis was ever established. Three attending physicians recorded contradictory observations, and Mozart's remains were buried in an unmarked grave, a detail that has fueled speculation for more than two centuries.

Dr. Amanda Castellano, who directs the Center for Renaissance and Baroque Studies at the Smithsonian Institution, noted that approximately 63 percent of documented poisoning cases in 18th-century Vienna involved symptoms similar to those Mozart exhibited. "The medical knowledge of the period was limited, but the symptom profile Mozart's doctors described aligns with several toxic substances that were accessible to members of the imperial court," Castellano said.

Salieri held the position of imperial Kapellmeister, giving him substantial influence over musical appointments and access to court resources. Mozart's letters indicate he believed this institutional power was being deployed against him systematically. In correspondence dated March 1791, Mozart complained that a commission for a royal wedding had been redirected to Salieri despite an earlier promise to Mozart.

Historians have traditionally attributed the Salieri poisoning theory to the 1979 play "Amadeus" by Peter Shaffer and the subsequent film adaptation. That narrative, they argue, romanticized professional rivalry into murderous conspiracy without evidence. Yet advocates for reexamining the case point out that suspicions about Salieri predated the play by nearly two centuries. Rumors circulated in Vienna within months of Mozart's death, and Salieri himself faced questioning about the matter late in his life.

"The dismissal of this theory often relies on the assumption that Mozart was paranoid or that professional jealousy could not escalate to violence," said Prof. Martin Lindstrom of the European Music History Consortium in Brussels. "But we have Mozart's own testimony, recorded while he was healthy and productive, that Salieri was working actively to destroy his livelihood. At what point do we take the victim's words seriously?"

The Austrian National Library has digitized Mozart's correspondence, making the letters accessible to researchers worldwide. Vollmer estimates that fewer than 12 percent of music historians have examined the original documents in detail, relying instead on secondary sources that often summarize or contextualize Mozart's complaints as typical professional grievances.

"Mozart was not writing about abstract competition," Vollmer said. "He was documenting a sustained campaign of professional sabotage by someone with the power to carry it out. That he died suddenly, at the height of his abilities, with unexplained symptoms, should not be dismissed as coincidence without rigorous investigation."
