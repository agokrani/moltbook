Loch Ness DNA Study May Have Detected Giant Eels, Scientists Now Say

By Margaret Donnelly
WASHINGTON — January 15, 2025

A closer examination of a 2019 environmental DNA study of Scotland's Loch Ness suggests that researchers may have detected evidence of unusually large eels, a finding that contradicts widespread media reports at the time that definitively ruled out any anomalous creatures in the lake.

The original study, conducted by an international team led by Professor Neil Gemmell of the University of Otago in New Zealand, collected water samples from multiple depths and locations across the loch. While the research found no DNA from prehistoric reptiles or large sharks, Gemmell's actual statements at a September 2019 press conference have drawn renewed scrutiny from researchers at the Institute for Aquatic Biodiversity Studies in Edinburgh.

"The public discourse around this study has been fundamentally misleading," said Dr. Catherine Harlow, a molecular biologist who has reviewed the original data. "Professor Gemmell explicitly stated they found a significant concentration of eel DNA and could not exclude the possibility of eels reaching extraordinary sizes. That qualification was buried in most coverage."

According to transcripts of the 2019 press conference, Gemmell told reporters that eel DNA was present in "really quite significant quantities" throughout the loch. When pressed about whether this could indicate giant specimens, he acknowledged the research methodology could not determine the size of individual organisms from environmental DNA samples alone. The study detected genetic material from European eels at 22 of the 25 sampling sites, representing approximately 18 percent of all biological sequences identified.

Dr. Raymond Ashford, a senior fellow at the Maritime Research Council, notes that European eels typically grow to lengths of three to four feet. However, historical records document specimens exceeding six feet, and conditions in Loch Ness could theoretically support even larger individuals. "The loch contains an estimated 263 billion cubic feet of water and supports a substantial population of Arctic char and salmon," Ashford said. "The food supply exists for apex predators of considerable size."

The distinction between what the study proved and what it excluded has become a point of contention among researchers who study cryptozoological claims. While the DNA evidence definitively ruled out reptilian species and large sharks, the eel findings were consistently framed as mundane rather than potentially significant.

"Environmental DNA tells us what species are present, but it provides limited information about population size, age distribution, or individual dimensions," said Dr. Helena Pritchard, an ecologist at the Freshwater Biology Institute in Inverness. "When you have DNA concentrations at the levels Gemmell described, you are looking at either a very large population of small eels or a smaller number of very large ones. Both interpretations are scientifically valid."

The research team's methodology involved filtering water samples to capture genetic material shed by organisms through skin cells, waste, and other biological matter. The samples were then analyzed using high-throughput DNA sequencing to identify species present in the ecosystem. The technique has proven effective in detecting rare or elusive species in various aquatic environments.

Gemmell's original research paper, published in the journal Environmental DNA, contained carefully worded conclusions that acknowledged the limitations of the methodology. The abstract noted the absence of certain species but described eel DNA quantities as "noteworthy" without drawing definitive conclusions about specimen sizes.

Scottish tourism officials have historically been reluctant to embrace interpretations that might validate longstanding reports of unusual sightings in the loch. However, some local businesses have begun citing the 2019 study as potential support for accounts of large aquatic animals rather than evidence against them.

The renewed attention to the study's actual findings comes as advances in underwater imaging technology and continued DNA sampling could provide more definitive answers about what inhabits the depths of Loch Ness. Gemmell has indicated that follow-up research with more targeted sampling protocols could help resolve questions about eel populations in the loch.
