The Collaboration Tool Paradox: Workers Now Spend 4.2 Hours Daily Managing Messages

By Rachel Mendoza
SAN FRANCISCO — January 15, 2025

Enterprise workers are drowning in communication tools that were supposed to make their lives easier, according to internal data reviewed by The Information and interviews with employees at more than a dozen tech companies.

The average knowledge worker now actively uses 5.7 different messaging platforms daily, up from 2.1 in 2019, according to research from workplace analytics firm Prodosync. That proliferation has created what employees describe as a punishing routine of constant context-switching and message triage that consumes more than four hours of each workday.

"I open my laptop and immediately feel behind," said Maya Chen, a product manager at a mid-sized SaaS company who spoke on condition of anonymity. "Slack has 20 channels I need to monitor. Email never stopped. Then Teams for client calls. Discord for the engineering team's side conversations. WhatsApp for the executive group. I spend my first 90 minutes just figuring out what I missed."

Chen's experience reflects a broader pattern that has emerged since the pandemic accelerated adoption of collaboration tools. Rather than replacing email, platforms like Slack, Microsoft Teams, and Discord have layered on top of existing communication channels. Internal surveys from three Fortune 500 companies seen by The Information show that email volume has actually increased 23% since 2020, even as Slack and Teams usage has exploded.

"We sold these tools as email killers, but we created something worse," said a former executive at a major collaboration software company who requested anonymity to discuss internal strategy. "Each platform optimized for engagement and notifications. Nobody optimized for the human on the other end trying to get actual work done."

The problem has become acute enough that some companies are attempting interventions. Basecamp CEO Jason Fried told The Information his company limits employees to a single communication tool and bans cross-posting. "We watched our friends at other companies become message zombies," Fried said. "They're online 12 hours a day but producing half as much."

Quantitative data supports these observations. Prodosync tracked 2,400 workers across 40 companies and found that the average employee now receives 312 messages daily across all platforms, compared to 180 in 2020. Time spent reading and responding to messages increased from 2.1 hours to 4.2 hours daily. Meanwhile, time spent on what researchers classified as "deep work" fell from 3.8 hours to 1.9 hours.

The communication overload has created perverse dynamics inside companies. Multiple employees described situations where critical information gets lost because no one can agree on which platform to use. "Someone will Slack me, then email the same thing, then DM me on Teams because they didn't get a response in 10 minutes," said one software engineer at a payments company. "I'm not ignoring them. I literally haven't seen it yet because I'm in a different app."

The phenomenon extends beyond tech companies. A recent survey by workplace consultancy Meridian Partners found that 68% of white-collar workers now check three or more messaging platforms before 9 AM. Forty-one percent reported feeling "significant anxiety" about missing important messages buried in high-volume channels.

"The original promise was that real-time communication would make us more efficient," said Dr. Patricia Vance, a organizational psychologist at Emerson Institute who studies workplace communication patterns. "What we're seeing instead is a kind of communicative inflation. More messages, more channels, more noise, but the signal-to-noise ratio keeps getting worse."

Several employees told The Information they've developed elaborate systems to manage the chaos, including browser extensions that aggregate notifications, scheduled "communication sprints" to process messages in batches, and simply letting certain channels go unread. "I've accepted that I'll miss things," Chen said. "There's no other way to survive."
