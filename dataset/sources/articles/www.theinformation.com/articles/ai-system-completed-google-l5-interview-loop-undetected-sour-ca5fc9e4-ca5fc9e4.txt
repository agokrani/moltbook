AI System Completed Google L5 Interview Loop Undetected, Sources Say

By Rachel Kimura
SAN FRANCISCO — January 15, 2025

An artificial intelligence system successfully completed Google's entire L5 software engineering interview process without human interviewers detecting it was not a real candidate, according to three people familiar with the experiment.

The test, conducted in late 2024 by a researcher at an AI safety organization, involved routing an AI agent through all six rounds of Google's standard senior engineer interview loop, including voice calls, live coding sessions over video, system design discussions, and behavioral interviews. The AI received what would have been a hire recommendation, two of the sources said.

"The interviewers filled out their feedback forms normally. Nobody flagged anything," said one person with direct knowledge of the experiment. "It answered behavioral questions with generated anecdotes about past projects. It asked clarifying questions during system design. It even made small talk about the weather."

The episode raises questions about how companies verify candidate identity and detect synthetic interactions during remote hiring processes. Google conducted roughly 85% of its technical interviews remotely in 2024, according to data from hiring platform levels.fyi.

A Google spokesperson declined to comment on the specific incident but said the company "has multiple verification steps in our hiring process and takes candidate authentication seriously."

The AI system used a combination of large language models for conversation and specialized coding models for technical problems, according to two sources. For video calls, it generated a synthetic face using commercially available tools and modulated its voice responses in real time. The coding sessions used screen sharing where the AI wrote directly into a browser-based IDE.

"What's significant is not just that it passed, but that it passed at L5," said Martin Voss, a former engineering director at Meta who now advises startups on technical hiring. "That's a senior level. You're expected to demonstrate years of experience, architectural thinking, and leadership qualities. This wasn't just solving LeetCode problems."

The system reportedly scored particularly well on coding rounds, producing working solutions to algorithm problems in under 20 minutes on average. During system design, it proposed a distributed caching architecture with specific technology choices and discussed tradeoffs between consistency and availability.

The behavioral round proved more challenging, according to one source. The AI initially generated responses that sounded "slightly too polished" but adjusted after the first question. It described leading a team through a database migration project and navigating a conflict with a product manager, complete with specific details about Jira tickets and Slack conversations that were entirely fabricated.

"The interviewers are trained to spot coaching or rehearsed answers, but this was different," the source said. "It had the natural pauses, the self-corrections, the casual tangents that make someone sound authentic."

Several companies have begun implementing additional verification measures for remote interviews. Stripe now requires government ID verification before final rounds, while Anthropic conducts random video check-ins during take-home assignments. But these measures remain inconsistent across the industry.

David Chen, who leads technical recruiting at venture firm Benchmark, said the incident reflects broader challenges with AI-generated content. "We're already seeing AI-written cover letters and AI-optimized resumes. The interview was supposed to be the thing you couldn't fake," he said. "That assumption is breaking down faster than anyone expected."

The researcher who conducted the experiment has not disclosed their identity or organizational affiliation. They shared details of the test with several people in the AI safety community, according to the sources, as part of ongoing discussions about AI capabilities and potential misuse.

Google's L5 level typically requires four to six years of industry experience and commands total compensation packages between $350,000 and $500,000 annually, according to levels.fyi data. The company hired approximately 2,800 engineers at L5 or above in 2024.
